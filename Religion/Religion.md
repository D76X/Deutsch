# What if as a catholic you realise that you are no longer interested in the thingss of the world, such as career, money, success and so on and only care about God but you have family responsibilities? What do you do then?

If you experience a profound shift where you lose all interest in worldly success but have a family to support, your Catholic faith calls you to [reframe your family responsibilities as your primary path to God](https://catholicstand.com/know-gods-will-careers-gods-call/#:~:text=Careers%20must%20be%20assessed,sacrifices.), rather than an obstacle to Him. In Catholic theology, this experience is often a grace called spiritual detachment (holy indifference to worldly things), but it must not be confused with neglecting your vocational duties. [1, 2] 
Here is how you can navigate this spiritual transition while honoring your commitments:
## 1. Reconceptualise Work as Stewardship
You do not need to care about "climbing the corporate ladder" or accumulating luxury to be a good provider. Shift your mindset from ambition to stewardship and service: [1, 2] 

* 
* Work as Penance and Prayer: Saint John Paul II frequently wrote about the "Gospel of Work." Going to a job you no longer value for its worldly prestige, simply to feed and shelter your children, is a profound act of sacrificial love. Offer your daily work up to God as a prayer.
* Detached Provision: You can work efficiently and earn money without loving the money itself. View your income strictly as a tool provided by God to care for the souls He entrusted to you. [1, 2] 
* 

## 2. Live the "Little Way" in the Home
For a married person with children, your family is your monastery. Your spouse and children are the primary people God has asked you to help get to Heaven.

* 
* Look to Saint Thérèse of Lisieux’s "Little Way"—doing ordinary, mundane tasks (like doing the dishes, packing lunches, or paying bills) with extraordinary love for God.
* Your family responsibilities are not distracting you from your holiness; they are the very school of your holiness.
* 

## 3. Adopt a Spirit of Evangelical Poverty
Since worldly success and status no longer matter to you, you have a beautiful opportunity to lead your family toward a more Christ-centered lifestyle: [2] 

* 
* Simplify your life: If agreed upon with your spouse, step back from high-stress, high-hour corporate roles in exchange for a simpler job that pays the bills but allows more time for prayer, family, and parish life. [1] 
* Practice radical charity: Distribute excess income to the poor or the Church once your family’s essential needs (housing, food, healthcare, and education) are securely met. [2] 
* 

## 4. Discern via Spiritual Direction
A sudden loss of interest in the world can sometimes look like spiritual growth, but it can also occasionally overlap with psychological burnout or a spiritual trial known as acedia (spiritual sloth/despondency).

* 
* Find a faithful Catholic priest or spiritual director.
* They can help you discern whether God is calling you to a hidden life of deep prayer within your current state of life, or if you are experiencing a transition that requires careful boundaries so your family does not feel abandoned by your withdrawal from worldly interests.
* 

## 5. Cultivate a Hidden Prayer Life
You do not need to enter a monastery to live entirely for God. You can cultivate a monastic heart:

* 
* Wake up early for silent mental prayer or a [Holy Hour](https://www.google.com/search?q=Holy+Hour&kgmid=/m/026pnbn) before the household wakes.
* Pray the Rosary during your daily commute.
* Practice the Presence of God throughout your workday, treating every person you encounter as Christ. [3] 
*

This experience is a profound spiritual state in Catholic theology known as detachment or "holy indifference," 
famously written about by saints like `St. Ignatius of Loyola` and `St. John of the Cross`. 

It is a sign of spiritual growth, but when paired with family responsibilities, it requires balancing two distinct 
Catholic callings: 

- your personal relationship with God and 
- your sacramental duties as a spouse or parent

In Catholic moral theology, you cannot abandon your primary vocation (your family) to pursue an individual spiritual ideal. 
Instead, you are called to find God through your responsibilities.

Here is how you can navigate this spiritual transition practically and faithfully.

# 1. Reframe Your Work as a Sacrifice of Love

When career, money, and success lose their worldly appeal, you no longer work for your own glory, pride, or ambition. 
Instead, your daily labor becomes an act of servant leadership and charity.

• Provide, don't accumulate: You do not need to climb the corporate ladder for status, but you must provide for your family’s housing, food, education, and healthcare.
• Offer it up: St. Josemaría Escrivá taught that everyday work can be sanctified. You can turn a boring or taxing job into a continuous prayer by offering your labor up for the souls of your children, your spouse, or those in need.

2. Practice "Detachment in the Midst of the World"
You do not need to enter a monastery to live a life dead to the world. You can live what St. Francis de Sales called the devout life in the secular world.
• Be in the world, not of it: Continue your job and family life, but maintain an interior freedom. If you get a promotion, view it strictly as a tool to better serve your family and the poor, rather than a boost to your ego.
• Simplify your life: Talk with your spouse about downscaling material desires. Lowering your overhead expenses might eventually allow you to take a less demanding, more service-oriented job that gives you more time for prayer and family.
3. See Your Family as Your Primary Monastery
In Catholicism, the family is called the Domestic Church. Your spouse and children are not obstacles to your holiness; they are the primary means by which God intends to sanctify you.
• Serve Christ in them: When you pack a lunch, help with homework, or listen to your spouse, you are directly serving Christ. Matthew 25 ("Whatsoever you do to the least of my brethren") applies first and foremost to your dependents.
• Lead them spiritually: Use your newfound fervor to cultivate a rich spiritual life at home. Lead family rosaries, read scripture together, and introduce your family to the beauty of the faith.
4. Seek Spiritual Direction and Discernment
A sudden loss of interest in daily life can sometimes be a spiritual trial (like a "Dark Night of the Senses") or, on a human level, a sign of burnout or depression.
• Find a holy priest or spiritual director: Do not make major, disruptive life changes without guidance. A spiritual director can help you discern whether this feeling is a call to deeper prayer or a temptation to neglect your duties under the guise of piety (a spiritual trap sometimes called quietism or false detachment).

What is the Apostolic Pardon?

An Apostolic Pardon is a special Catholic blessing given by a priest to a person who is dying or in danger of death, granting a plenary indulgence that removes all temporal punishment due to sin.
What is the Apostolic Pardon?
• Definition: A special blessing with an attached plenary indulgence (the complete removal of temporal punishment left after sins are forgiven).
• Difference from Confession: Confession forgives the eternal guilt and punishment of sins, while the Apostolic Pardon wipes away the remaining temporal consequences or "debt" of sin that would otherwise require purification.
• Timing: Given at the end of life, usually during the Last Rites after the Sacrament of Reconciliation (Confession), the Anointing of the Sick, and Viaticum (Holy Communion for the dying).
When and How it is Administered
• When needed: Reserved for individuals who are seriously ill, on their deathbed, or facing imminent danger of death.
• If a priest is absent: Most users on Reddit agree that while the formal sacramental blessing requires a priest with proper faculties, Catholics who are truly repentant and trust in God's mercy can still receive a generalized final grace at the hour of death.
The Prayer
A priest administering the blessing uses words given by the Apostolic See, such as:
"By the authority which the Apostolic See has given me, I grant you a full pardon and the remission of all your sins in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen."
